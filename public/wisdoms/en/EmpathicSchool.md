# The School of Love
## Soft Structures and Learning Through Feeling

The deep longing for an educational system that is not based on the rigid transmission of knowledge, but on joy, love, and genuine experience, requires a radical shift in perspective. In the "School of Love," the methodology is adapted so that abstract concepts become tangible through feeling and action. The traditional classroom gives way to a space of encounter, in which drama pedagogy becomes the central pillar of learning.

Subjects such as art, history, music, and religion merge organically in theatrical play. History is not learned chronologically by rote, but reenacted and experienced. Students explore the motivations and emotional spirit of past eras firsthand. Following this historical thread, the natural sciences—physics, chemistry, and mathematics—are introduced precisely when they fit naturally into the historical or content-related context.

### Empathy for Things and Living Mathematics
Even abstract disciplines are brought to life through play. In the theater, fundamental feelings and empathy for others can be practiced, but also—however unusual it may sound at first—for "things" and concepts. When a child plays Newton's apple and symbolically falls to the ground, they embody gravity. Mathematics, from simple addition and subtraction to more complex fractions, is integrated visually on stage. The practical application of knowledge becomes immediately tangible.

Children also learn foreign languages intuitively by acting out everyday scenes, such as shopping in a foreign market. It's not about grammatical perfection, but about developing a fearless feel for the language—just enough words to greet each other while traveling and order one's favorite food.

### Yoga as Morning Grounding
Such an intensive, empathetic school day requires special preparation. Before the children slip into the diverse roles of the world theater, the morning begins with yoga. This practice of mindfulness connects body and breath, creating fertile ground for the empathy needed in the rest of the lessons. The children arrive fully in the present moment, tuning themselves internally like instruments before a concert and centering themselves. The exercises can even be thematically adapted to the curriculum—grounding, weighty postures when studying gravity in physics, or flowing movements when learning about seafaring.

### The Space Without Grades: Intrinsic Motivation
When the external pressure of grades and evaluations is removed, the children's innate, intrinsic motivation is reawakened. In the School of Love, they don't learn for grades, but from the pure joy of creating together. The drive arises from the responsibility for the theater ensemble, from the tangible meaning of the knowledge—because they want to apply it directly in the performance—and from the inner experience of their own growth.

### Silent Roles: Everyone Finds Their Place
A system based on love forces no one into the spotlight. If a child withdraws or doesn't want to be on stage, this resistance is understood as a valuable need for security. For these children, there are essential "silent roles." They can document the performance as appreciative observers, design the set backstage, or calculate the trajectory of the apple being played as researchers in the background. In this way, every child remains a supported, essential part of the community.

### Being Seen and "Soft Structures"
Perhaps the strongest foundation of this school is the feeling of being seen and understood in one's essence—this applies to students as well as teachers. There are no rigid dogmas here, but rather the concept of "soft structures." When a teacher introduces an established method, such as an experiment description model, into a lesson, this is done without preconceived notions. The class re-examines the tool, questions it, and works out its meaning for themselves. Perhaps the group even develops an even better structure. Through this inquiry-based approach, no one loses their love of language or science. A deep understanding of "why" strengthens children from within and makes them resilient—ready to face even the toughest structures of the outside world later with self-confidence and genuine understanding.
