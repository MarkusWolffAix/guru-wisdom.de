# The Voices of the World
## A family tree of language mothers

When we consider the fascinating landscape of human languages, we are looking at a vast, sprawling tree. What linguistics often refers to as Proto-Indo-European (PIE) is the great mother tongue for almost half the world's population. It sent its children from India to Iceland, giving birth to languages that dominate our modern world—including English, German, Spanish, Russian, Persian (Farsi) in present-day Iran, and the ancient, sacred Sanskrit.

But this Indo-European mother is not alone. She is part of a remarkable council of mothers, reflecting the diversity of human expression in all its facets. Humanity has not drawn from a single source to give meaning to the world, but has kindled many fires of communication.

Among the great sisters of Proto-Indo-European are powerful proto-languages that shaped vast swathes of the earth: The Sino-Tibetan mother is the cradle of the East Asian languages, whose most famous offspring is Mandarin. From the Afro-Asiatic mother sprang the languages of prophets and pharaohs – Arabic, Hebrew, ancient Egyptian, and Aramaic. The Niger-Congo family forms the most sonorous and prolific lineage in the world, traversing sub-Saharan Africa with languages such as Swahili and Zulu. The Austronesian mother, in turn, is the great seafarer, whose descendants crossed oceans from Madagascar to the Māori in New Zealand. And the Dravidian mother represents an ancient, deep root in southern India that flourished long before the Indo-European languages reached the subcontinent.

### The great mothers of languages, their origins and their global legacy

To make these immense currents comprehensible, we consider them in their chronological and present-day presence. This reveals when these roots were likely put down and how unequally the legacy of our voices is distributed today:

| The mother language (proto-language) | Estimated origin | Today's heirs (examples) | share of the world population |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Proto-Indo-European** | ca. 4500 – 2500 BC | English, Spanish, Hindi, Persian (Iran), German | **approximately 46.0%** |
| **Ursino-Tibetan** | ca. 4000 – 3000 BC | Mandarin (Chinese), Cantonese, Tibetan | **approximately 18.5%** |
| **Urniger-Congo** | ca. 10,000 – 5,000 BC | Swahili, Yoruba, Zulu, Igbo | **approximately 7.5%** |
| **Proto-Afroasiatic** | ca. 10,000 – 8,000 BC | Arabic, Hebrew, Hausa, Amharic | **approximately 7.0%** |
| **Uraustronesian** | ca. 3000 BC | Indonesian, Tagalog, Malay, Maori | **approximately 5.0%** |
| **Urdravidian** | ca. 3000 – 1500 BC | Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam, Kannada | **approximately 4.0%** |
| **Proto-Turkic / Altaic** | ca. 2500 – 1500 BC | Turkish, Uzbek, Kazakh | **approximately 2.5%** |
| **Ururalic** | ca. 4000 – 2000 BC | Hungarian, Finnish, Estonian | **approximately 0.4%** |
| **Other / Isolated** | Variable | Japanese, Korean, Vietnamese, Basque | **approximately 9.1%** |

The starry sky of human languages reveals far more than just these major currents. There are the quiet, deeply rooted mothers like Uralic, from which Finnish and Hungarian emerged, or the countless ancient linguistic lineages of the indigenous peoples of the Americas and Australia. And then there are the mysteries: the isolated ones. Languages like Basque in Europe or the Sumerian heritage of antiquity—monolithic languages without known relatives. They remind us that the spark of language creation is a spontaneous, profoundly human need to wrest meaning from the cosmos through sound.

A particularly poignant example of the deep kinship within the Indo-European family is the connection between Persian (Farsi) and Sanskrit. Both originate from the Indo-Iranian branch. The melodic, poetic quality of Farsi shares the same ancient source with the mantras of the Vedas. When we consider the words and sounds of both traditions, we discover not merely a linguistic shift, but a universal symbolism of sound. The phonetic architecture of these ancient languages reveals how breath and vibration were deliberately used to make spiritual truths tangible. Whether in Sufism or Vedic rites, the sound patterns reflect a collective, ancient understanding of being and transcendence.

The world map of languages is therefore not just a directory of vocabulary, but an archive of the human mind – shaped by time, migrations, wind and the perpetual search for connection.
