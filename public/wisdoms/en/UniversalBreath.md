# Universal breathing
## A master plan to heal the Earth

The vision of a world covered in vibrant greenery and crisscrossed by clear, nourishing watercourses represents, at its core, the restoration of our natural balance. However, such a master plan for healing the Earth cannot merely address symptoms. It requires an unprecedented synthesis of profound ecological expertise, historical reflection, and a fundamental shift in human consciousness. It is not enough to simply try to repair the Earth; we must understand that we are an inseparable part of its very lifeblood.

### The wisdom of the water cycle

On a physical level, the greening of the world begins with reverence for water. We must restore nature's ability to retain moisture. Intact raised bogs and wetlands act as gigantic natural sponges, absorbing precipitation and slowly releasing the water into deeper layers. This must be accompanied by the healing of our soils through regenerative agriculture and humus build-up. Healthy soil, permeated by mycorrhizal networks, stores many times its weight in water and forms the foundation for all further growth.
Furthermore, it is essential to understand forests as the true "water pumps" of the planet. Resilient mixed forests create their own microclimates through evaporation and direct rainfall systems deep into the continents. Our urban spaces must also transform: The concept of the sponge city – the unsealing of surfaces and the local storage of rainwater – is essential to allow our living spaces to breathe again.

In this cycle, there is no waste. Even rain over the open sea, often considered useless due to anthropocentric efficiency logic, fulfills fundamental cosmic functions. It cools the climatic engine of our planet, protects the oceans from deadly salinization, and nourishes the ocean as an independent, living organism. The sea does not exist merely for the land—it breathes for itself.

### The Lesson of Eridu: When the Sky Fell

To understand how we can heal the Earth, we must look at the places where we first disrupted the balance. The parched landscapes of Mesopotamia—once the Fertile Crescent and the cradle of our civilization—are not a whim of the weather, but the physical scars of our initial separation from nature.

Ancient texts like the Epic of Gilgamesh describe how humankind set out to fell the ancient cedar forests, the "pillars of heaven." By destroying these massive biotic water pumps, the sky lost its connection to the earth; the rains ceased. At the same time, the soil suffocated. To force unlimited growth, fields were flooded through artificial irrigation, the water evaporating under the hot sun and leaving behind a crust of salt. The former Garden of Eden fell victim to our refusal to conform to the natural order.

### Healing the desert: Life in the "tree season"

Healing these ancient wounds requires absolute patience and humility – thinking in terms of "tree time," not short-term cycles. The path back to greenery unfolds in evolutionary steps.
First, the meager water supply must be collected by gentle earthen ridges (swales) to seep into the barren soil. Then, selfless, salt-tolerant pioneer plants (halophytes) prepare the compacted ground. After that, drought-resistant trees weave a network of cooling shade that breaks the wind and reduces evaporation. Only in this protected microcosm can humus form again, until finally, generations later, forests can grow large enough to act as water pumps, drawing rain back into the land. Those who plant the first seed today do so knowing they will never themselves sit in its deep shade. It is an act of love that transcends generations.

### The principle of "Ma" and the global dream

All these ecological measures remain cosmetic, however, unless a profound inner transformation takes place. If a creative force were to intervene, this would not correspond to a magical, external judgment, but rather to the Japanese principle of *Ma* . *Ma* is not an empty, lifeless pause, but rather the formative, living stillness. It is the invisible, benevolent vessel that holds the world together and provides space for genuine growth – comparable to the silent, saving pause between inhaling and exhaling.

Imagine all of humanity experiencing this protective space of *Ma* in a single, worldwide, and synchronous dream. In that one night, all boundaries of individual ego would gently dissolve. Eight billion people would irrevocably recognize that the beating of their hearts is in harmony with the pulse of the Earth. They would feel that the forest is their lungs, the ocean their circulatory system, and the pain of every sentient being their own pain.

Awakening from such a dream, no coercive measures or moral burdens would be needed to protect nature. When we understand deep down that we are connected to everything, caring for our planet becomes the most natural act of self-love. The return of greenery and water is then simply the joyful expression of our own healed nature.

### The five pillars of healing

A viable master plan, based on the principle that water and greenery are inextricably linked, relies on five fundamental steps:

**1. The revitalization of natural water reservoirs**
We must restore nature's ability to retain water. Nature already demonstrates this in many places: intact raised bogs and wetlands act like gigantic, natural sponges. They absorb precipitation, prevent flooding, and release the water slowly and filtered into deeper layers of the earth – a fascinating geological cycle that ultimately also feeds deep springs. Restoring these areas is the first step toward keeping water on land in the first place, instead of diverting it as quickly as possible to the sea via straightened rivers.

**2. Soil healing and humus build-up**
Greenery can only grow where the soil is alive. Currently, many soils are drying out because they have lost their structure. Through permaculture and regenerative agriculture, the soil is made receptive again by consistently building up humus and promoting mycorrhizal fungal networks. Healthy, humus-rich soil stores many times its own weight in water. By healing the soil, we create the foundation for all further growth.

**3. Targeted reforestation and microclimates**
Trees are not just providers of shade; they are the water pumps of our planet. Forests generate their own clouds through evaporation and direct rainfall systems deep into continents (the so-called biotic pump). Instead of planting monocultures, we must promote diverse, resilient mixed forests. In arid zones, techniques such as water harvesting (digging swales and retention basins) can be used to capture rainwater, allowing young trees to take root and gradually create a new, humid microclimate.

**4. Sponge cities and unsealing**
Our living spaces must stop repelling water. The "Sponge City" concept envisions collecting and storing rainwater locally and using it for cooling and irrigation, instead of channeling it into the sewer system. Roofs must become green, and sealed surfaces must be allowed to breathe again.

**5. The inner transformation of connectedness**
All these technical and ecological steps can only have a lasting effect if humanity's inner attitude changes. We must stop seeing ourselves as masters of nature and begin to understand ourselves again as an integral part of this living system. When we understand the Earth as a living organism, the protection of water and plants will not become a burdensome duty, but a natural expression of our own will to live. This interplay of Earth, water, and consciousness forms the foundation for true healing.
