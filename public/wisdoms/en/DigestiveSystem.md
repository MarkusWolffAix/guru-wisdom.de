# Our digestive system
## Agni and the Fire of Life

The digestive system is one of the most complex and resource-intensive networks in the human body. It serves not only the purely mechanical and chemical processing of food, but also functions as our largest immune organ and as a "second brain" (the enteric nervous system). The digestive process does not begin in the stomach, but is initiated in the so-called *cephalic phase* (head phase): The mere sight, smell, or even thought of food activates the vagus nerve via the central nervous system. This signals the liver, stomach, and pancreas to produce digestive juices and enzymes early on, in order to prepare the body for the upcoming food intake.

### 1. The Path of Food: Detailed Anatomy and Physiology

**Mouth and throat: The starting point of digestion**
The first major transformation phase of food takes place in the oral cavity – both mechanically and chemically.
* **Glands and saliva production:** The three major pairs of salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, and sublingual glands) produce up to 1.5 liters of saliva daily. This protein-rich saliva lubricates food (for bite formation) and simultaneously protects the teeth and oral mucosa.
* **Enzymatic predigestion:** Chemical carbohydrate digestion begins immediately through the enzyme *alpha-amylase* (ptyalin). It breaks down complex, long-chain carbohydrates (such as starch from bread or potatoes) into shorter sugar compounds. This is why bread tastes sweet after prolonged chewing.
* **The systemic significance of chewing (mastication):** Extensive chewing is far more than just grinding. It is an active mechanism for stress reduction: The rhythmic jaw movement stimulates brain regions that lead to a measurable reduction in the stress hormone cortisol in the blood. Furthermore, blood flow to the brain increases, noticeably improving cognitive attention and alertness. On an anatomical level, intensive chewing stimulates the entire jaw and facial musculature. This promotes optimal jaw development, especially during growth phases, prevents misaligned teeth, and, through a wide jaw structure, ensures permanently open, unobstructed airways.

**Esophagus and stomach: Transport and disinfection**
The swallowing reflex is a highly complex process that closes the airways and directs food into the esophagus.
* **Transport:** The esophagus is not a passive descending tube, but an active muscular tube. Through highly coordinated, rhythmic wave-like movements (peristalsis), the food bolus (chyme) is transported reliably to the stomach, even when lying down or standing on one's head.
* **Stomach function:** The stomach resembles a muscular, flexible pouch that serves as an intermediate storage container and intensively kneads the food through strong muscle contractions. 
* **Gastric juice and chemical barrier:** The parietal cells of the gastric mucosa produce highly acidic hydrochloric acid (pH 1.5 to 2). This acidic environment is an essential barrier of the immune system, as it kills bacteria, viruses, and parasites ingested with food. At the same time, the chief cells activate the enzyme *pepsin* , which breaks down and denatures proteins.

**The small intestine: The center of absorption**
The small intestine is the most important organ for the absorption of macro- and micronutrients into the bloodstream. It is up to 6 meters long and possesses a gigantic internal surface area due to its ring-shaped folds, countless villi (projections), and microvilli (microscopic protrusions on the cells). It is divided into three specialized sections:
1. **Duodenum:** This approximately 24 cm long, C-shaped section connects directly to the stomach. Its main function is to neutralize the extremely acidic chyme from the stomach. The excretory ducts of the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas empty into the duodenum.
2. **Jejunum:** At approximately 2 to 2.5 m in length, it is the primary site for the enzymatic breakdown and subsequent absorption of carbohydrates, proteins, and water-soluble vitamins into the bloodstream. (The name "jejunum" derives from its anatomy, as it is usually found empty after death.)
3. **Ileum:** This approximately 3-meter-long, highly convoluted, muscular, hollow organ section is highly specialized. It is not only responsible for the absorption of vitamin B12 (with the help of intrinsic factor from the stomach) and the recycling of bile acids, but also contains Peyer's patches – a collection of lymph follicles that makes it a central site for local and systemic immune defense.

**The large intestine (colon) and anus: reabsorption and excretion**
In the large intestine, no significant nutrient absorption takes place. Its main function is to extract up to 1.5 liters of water and vital electrolytes (such as sodium and potassium) daily from the liquid chyme. This thickens the stool. Indigestible residues are collected in the rectum and eventually excreted through the anus.

### 2. Participating auxiliary bodies: The biochemical factories

The secretions of the large glands and accessory organs are absolutely essential for smooth digestion in the small intestine:

* **The liver (hepar):** As the body's largest metabolic organ, it not only filters the blood but also continuously produces bile. Bile is responsible for the "emulsification" of fats – it breaks down large fat droplets into tiny droplets so that digestive enzymes can break them down.
* **The gallbladder (vesica biliaris):** It is the liver's reservoir. It stores and concentrates the bile produced (approximately 30 to 80 ml). When fatty food enters the duodenum, the gallbladder contracts and releases the concentrated bile.
* **The pancreas:** An absolutely key organ. Its exocrine part produces up to 2 liters of digestive juice daily, brimming with enzymes for breaking down fats (lipases), carbohydrates (amylases), and proteins (proteases). It also produces large quantities of bicarbonate, which is essential for neutralizing the harsh stomach acid in the duodenum and protecting the intestinal lining from damage.
* **The intestinal mucosa (epithelium):** The innermost layer of the intestine is not only an absorption surface, but also contains tiny glands (crypts) that secrete their own digestive fluids and mucus to ensure further transport.

### 3. The microbiome and the gut-brain axis: Our internal command center

An estimated 38 trillion microorganisms (bacteria, fungi, viruses, archaea) live in the human large intestine. This gigantic ecosystem, the microbiome, weighs up to 2 kilograms. It serves far more than just the fermentation of dietary fiber; it is a biochemical command center that modulates the entire body.

**Saturation, immune system and disease defense**
* **Hormonal satiety regulation:** Stretch receptors and peptide messengers in the gastrointestinal tract regulate the feeling of hunger to the brain. The microbiome actively intervenes in this process: Certain strains of bacteria produce proteins that mimic our satiety hormones. Some strains can signal that one is still mistakenly feeling hungry. Other bacteria, such as specific E. coli strains, can specifically reduce cravings for sweets because they themselves use sugar building blocks for their cell walls and signal to the body that its needs have been met.
* **Immune defense and allergies (e.g., asthma):** The microbiome trains our immune system. When certain types of bacteria ferment (break down) indigestible dietary fiber, short-chain fatty acids (such as butyrate and propionate) are produced. These valuable fatty acids reach the bone marrow via the bloodstream, among other places, where they influence the production of specific immune cells (macrophages). These newly formed immune cells migrate via the bloodstream to the lungs. There, they have a strong anti-inflammatory effect and can significantly suppress excessive immune responses—such as those that occur in asthma, allergies, or autoimmune diseases. In general, a diverse gut flora strengthens the overall defense against viral infections like colds and flu.
* **Dietary influence on diversity:** The composition of bacteria is highly malleable. A Western diet high in fat and sugar, as well as highly processed foods, causes important bacterial strains to die off and severely restricts the diversity of the microbiome. A fiber-rich plant-based diet, on the other hand, acts as "fertilizer" (prebiotics) and promotes a resilient microbiome.

**Control of emotions, motivation and psyche (The gut-brain axis)**
The gut and brain exchange millions of signals every second. The main information highway for this is the **vagus nerve** (the 10th cranial nerve), which runs directly from the brainstem to the abdomen. Interestingly, 80 to 90 percent of the vagus nerve's nerve fibers run through the gut.
