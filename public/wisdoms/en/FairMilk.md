# Fair Milk
## Conscious nutrition for body and mind

The milk yield per cow has been massively increased through breeding and concentrated feed, pushing the animals to their physical limits.
* **Natural state:** A mother cow produces approximately **4–8 liters** per day solely for her calf.
* **100 years ago (around 1920):** An average cow gave approximately **7–10 liters** per day.
* **Today (high performance):** The average milk consumption in Germany is around **30 liters** per day. Top cows reach over **60 liters** , and the world record is around **127.6 liters** .
* **The ideal healthy approach:** A yield of **15–25 liters** allows the cow to grow old healthily and meet its energy needs from grass and hay.

## 2. Geographical roots: Holstein & Friesland
The **Holstein-Friesian** breed originates from the coastal regions of the **Netherlands (Friesland)** and **Germany (Holstein)** .
* **Holstein:** Southern part of Schleswig-Holstein (between the Elbe and Eider rivers). Cities: **Kiel, Lübeck, Neumünster** . Distance from Hanover: approx. **150–250 km** .
* **Friesland:** Divided into the Dutch province **of Fryslân** and the German district **of Friesland** (Jever, Varel).
* **Breeding history:** Bred for maximum quantity in the USA, they returned to Europe in the 20th century as "turbo cows".

## 3. The clear advantage: Why the long-lived cow is economically and ecologically better
The long-lived cow ( **12–15 years** lifespan) beats the high-performance cow ( **5–6 years** ) in all sustainability factors:

### Economic superiority:
* **Lower fixed costs:** The rearing costs of approximately **€2,500** are depreciated over 10–13 years instead of just 3 years. This significantly reduces the cost per liter of milk.
* **Higher profit:** Massive savings on **veterinary costs** (fewer inflammations/metabolic problems) and lower expenses for expensive imported feed.
* **Improved herd management:** The farmer has to raise fewer calves and can sell surplus animals instead of using them as expensive replacements for prematurely maturing animals.

### Ecological superiority:
* **CO2 efficiency & methane balance:** Considering the entire lifespan and the herd including offspring, the long-lived cow system produces significantly less methane per liter of milk. While a high-yielding cow often emits less methane per liter on a single day (dilution effect), this isolated picture is misleading: Because high-yielding cows "wear out" quickly, the farmer must constantly raise new calves. These young animals produce methane for two to three years without giving a single liter of milk. The long-lived cow massively eliminates these unproductive rearing phases.
* **Climate protection through grazing:** Long-lived cows graze on **permanent grassland** . This protects soils that store enormous amounts of CO2, while high-performance cows often require arable fodder (corn/soy), for the cultivation of which land must be plowed.

## 6. Why does the high-performance dairy cow even exist? (The systemic constraint)
If the long-lived cow is so superior, why does high-performance breeding dominate? Farmers don't choose this out of malice, but due to agricultural policy and economic conditions:
* **The payment system:** Dairies pay almost exclusively for the sheer **quantity** (liters). There is no "longevity bonus" on the regular market. Those who have bills to pay need liters in their tanks.
* **The vicious cycle ("grow or get out"):** With historically low milk prices, the survival strategy was: "If I hardly earn anything per liter, I have to produce more." This led to oversupply and further falling prices.
* **High fixed costs per stall:** Modern barns cost millions. A cow producing 15 liters of milk occupies the same expensive space as a cow producing 40 liters. Banks insist that the barn space generates maximum return to repay the loan.
* **Historical focus:** After the Second World War, the political motto was: "Maximum yields at favorable prices" in order to feed the growing population independently of imports.
* **Global availability of cheap feed:** The turbo-cow only functions through the massive import of extremely protein-rich concentrated feed (soy). 50 liters per day could not be produced from domestic grass alone.
* **One-sided breeding goals:** For decades, breeding associations blindly selected for "milk quantity", while neglecting health and fitness characteristics that are harder to measure.

## 7. Exiting the System (How Change Succeeds)
The shift away from "cheap milk" must take place on three levels: on the farm, in politics/economics, and with the consumer.

### A. The Transformation on the Farm (The Farmer)
The transition is a multi-year process known as **"extensification"** or **"low-input strategy"** :
* **Change breeding strategy:** away from pure Holstein genetics, towards "utility crosses" with robust breeds (Simmental, Jersey, Brown Swiss) for more fitness and hoof health.
* **Switching to a low-input feeding system:** Drastically reducing expensive imported concentrated feed. The cows primarily eat grass and hay again. Milk production decreases, but feed costs fall even more sharply.
* **Certification & Premium Markets:** Joining strong organic associations (Bioland, Demeter) or obtaining niche certification ("hay milk gtS", "pasture milk") to secure guaranteed price premiums.
* **Direct marketing:** Own farm dairies, milk filling stations or community-supported agriculture (CSA) to keep the added value on the farm.

### B. Systemic change (politics and supermarkets)
* **Restructuring of subsidies:** Taxpayer money (EU agricultural policy) must not be distributed as a lump sum based on area, but must reward ecosystem services (grazing, species protection, avoidance of concentrated feed).
* **True Cost Accounting:** The hidden environmental costs of conventional milk (nitrates in water, climate damage) must be factored into the price. Sustainable milk would then be the more economically viable option.
* **End of loss leaders:** The ban on selling food below production costs must be fully enforced. Milk must not be a cheap commodity.
* **Long-term contracts:** Dairies and retailers must offer farmers multi-year contracts with fixed prices that guarantee their livelihoods, in order to give them planning security for barn conversions.

### C. The influence of the consumer
* **Choose strong labels:** Associations such as Demeter, Bioland, Naturland or special labels such as "Pro Weideland" and "Heumilch gtS" guarantee better conditions than the EU organic label alone.
* **Pay attention to the housing system:** For conventional milk, choose at least level 3 (outdoor climate) or 4 (premium/organic), as this guarantees contact with the outdoors and more space.
* **The "hidden" milk:** When it comes to cheese, butter, yogurt and quark, also pay attention to organic or pasture-raised milk quality (this is where 80% of the milk produced ends up).
* **Regional & Direct:** Support milk filling stations, farm shops and concepts such as Community Supported Agriculture (CSA).

## 8. Practical example: The "low-input" farm in reality
A typical real-world scenario demonstrates how this exit strategy can be successful:

**The old system:** Farmer Müller kept 100 high-performance Holstein cows (10,000 liters/year per cow). They were kept indoors year-round, fed expensive soy/corn feed, and incurred high veterinary costs. After five years, the cows were exhausted. Müller worked 70 hours a week, and the profit was minimal.

**The change:** Müller is now crossbreeding in robust breeds (Simmental/Jersey) and has stopped buying concentrated feed. The cows graze on pasture from spring to autumn and eat hay in winter.

**The result (the new system):**
* **Milk production is decreasing:** only 6,000 liters instead of 10,000 liters per cow.
* **Profits increase:** Since grass grows almost for free, feed costs decrease by 70%. The animals are more robust, and veterinary costs are halved.
* **Long lifespan:** The cows live to be 10 to 12 years old; much less breeding is required.
* **Fair price:** Thanks to the hay milk/organic quality, Müller now receives 60 cents instead of 35 cents per liter from the dairy.

*Result:* Although 40% less milk is produced, the farmer has more profit, healthier animals, less workload and has successfully overcome the system's constraints.

---

## 9. Conclusion
Cheap milk is economically short-sighted and ecologically costly. Switching to long-lived cows, identifiable by labels like **Demeter, Bioland** , or **Animal Welfare Standard 4** , is the only way to achieve sustainable agriculture. A small price increase of around **30 cents** in stores ensures the survival of farmers, protects the climate, and safeguards animal health.
