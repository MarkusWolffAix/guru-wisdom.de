# The Mnemonic Device
## From Detours to Self-Knowledge

We often use the word "mnemonic device" casually in everyday life for small learning aids or mnemonic devices. But its true origin holds a profound metaphor about learning, respecting our own limitations, and the ways in which we overcome them.

To understand this, we must return to a time when animals were our everyday companions. Donkeys have a reputation for being stubborn. But this supposed "stubbornness" is, in reality, a deeply rooted, very clever survival instinct. A donkey strictly refuses to wade through a small body of water or a stream, even if the water is only ankle-deep. The reason for this lies in the nature of the water: Due to the reflection on the surface, the animal cannot see how deep the bottom is. Where the donkey cannot see for sure what it is stepping on, it stops. It does not trust the unknown.


To avoid forcing the animal—which is hardly possible anyway—people built small, very simple bridges over these waterways: the so-called "donkey bridge." It was a small detour, an artificial aid that allowed the animal to cross the obstacle with dry feet and, above all, without fear.

Our minds work in exactly the same way. Sometimes we stand before a piece of information, a formula, or an abstract concept like the donkey before the stream. Our minds refuse to take the direct route; they find no foothold, and the memory remains hazy. The donkey bridge in our thinking—be it a rhyme, an absurd image, or a short story—is this artificial detour. It relieves our minds of the weight of pure facts and builds a safe, easily navigable path to true understanding. The wisdom of the donkey bridge, therefore, lies not in the donkey's stupidity, but in the bridge builder's wise care. It's perfectly fine if the direct route is sometimes too difficult; we are allowed to create detours to reach our goal.

But this already beautiful story reveals yet another, far deeper dimension when we look more closely at the cause of the fear: the reflection in the water.

What if the donkey wasn't just ignorant of the water, but the reflection itself carried the true message? If you mirror the word "donkey," the word "read" appears.

This wonderful observation is a masterful play on words that reveals a philosophical truth: The donkey, gazing into the mirror of his own fear, finds there the call to knowledge. *Read.* The water, which, through its reflection, obscures his view of the bottom and freezes him, conceals no danger at all, but an invitation. When he pauses, looks into it, and recognizes himself there, ignorance transforms into understanding. "Reading"—whether in books, in the patterns of our own reactions, or in the realities of nature—is the key to dispelling the murky water.


Those who learn to read and understand the unknown soon no longer need artificial bridges, because they realize that the waters of life are not as deep and threatening as they first appear. External aids thus become an inner transformation through knowledge. Looking into the murky water becomes reading one's own soul.
