# The Body's Memory
## How the Etymology of Our Five Senses Reveals a Forgotten Worldview

Language is far more than a mere tool for transmitting information. It is a living archive, a deep memory of humankind, in which the experiences, worldviews, and moments of wonder of our most distant ancestors are preserved. When we speak today of our five senses—sight, smell, taste, touch, and hearing—we use words that have been polished over millennia like pebbles in a riverbed. Yet beneath their smooth, everyday surface lies an archaic power.

In the modern world, we often understand perception as a passive process: stimuli flood in on us, and our bodies register them. A look at the etymology, the origin of our words, however, paints a completely different picture. For the people of early Indo-European and Germanic times, perception was not a silent receiving, but an active movement out into the world. Even the overarching word **sense** (Old High German *sin*) traces back to the Indo-European root *sent-*, which originally meant "to take a direction, to follow a trail, to travel." To perceive with the senses, therefore, meant in the truest sense of the word, to set out on a journey and follow the traces of life.

### The Eye and Seeing: The Art of Tracking

Our most important gateway to the world is the **eye**. It originates from the Old High German *ouga* (from the 8th century) and the Germanic *augan-*, which in turn go back to the ancient Indo-European root *okʷ-* or *okū-* ("to see"). This root is so fundamental that it runs like a common thread through the European language family – from the Latin *oculus* through the Gothic *augō* to the English *eye*.


### The Eye and Seeing: The Art of Tracking Things get interesting when we consider the corresponding verb: **to see**. The trail leads us through Old High German *sehan* and Proto-Germanic *sehwan-* to the Indo-European root *sekʷ-*. Its original meaning was "to notice, to follow." It is closely related to the Latin *sequi* ("to follow"). For our ancestors, seeing was not a static fixation, but a dynamic accompaniment: The eye follows the trail of a migrating animal, the flight of a bird, or the course of the celestial bodies and *follows* them through space.

### The Nose and Smell: The Gateway to Life and the Night Watchman at the Fire

Hardly any word in the Indo-European language family has survived the millennia as unscathed as **nose**. From the Indo-European *nās-* through the Old Indian *nā́sā* and the Latin *nasus* to the Old High German *nasa* and the English *nose*, the sound of the word remained almost unchanged—like a rock in the surf of linguistic change (the word even lives on in geographical names for promontories like *Blankenese* or *Loch Ness*).

But even more fascinating than the age of the word is its original grammar: In the proto-language, *nās-* was in the dual form, the two-part form. Our ancestors didn't name the nose after the single visible cartilaginous protrusion on the face, but after the invisible cavity: **"the two nostrils"**. For ancient cultures, these two openings were the sacred gateways of the breath of life—whether as *Prana* in Indian Sanskrit, as the divine breath of life in the creation story, or as *Pneuma* and *Spiritus* in antiquity, where breath, mind, and soul were one and the same. The nose is the only threshold of our body that never closes.

It is precisely at this ever-open threshold that the profound mystery of the verb **to smell** is revealed. It originates from the Old High German *riohhan* (8th century) and the Proto-Germanic *\*reuk-a-*. Its original meaning was simply **"to smoke, to vaporize, to give off smoke"**—it is the direct linguistic sibling of our noun **smoke** (preserved in English as *to reek*).

But why did the word for the smoke of fire merge with our perception through the nose? The answer lies in the dark stillness of the night. When early humans slept in their caves or huts, all their senses withdrew: eyelids were closed, mouths sealed, and the waking mind extinguished. Only one sense kept tireless watch at the two open gateways of breath: the nose, connected via a direct nerve pathway to the oldest survival centers of our brain. If the vital hut fire threatened to go out or get out of control, it sent its invisible messenger through the darkness—smoke. Because the nose is the organ that remains active even in the unconsciousness of sleep and hears the fire's warning call, the word for "smoking" evolved during the Middle Ages into the word for our deepest, most unconscious sensory perception: "smelling."

While the nose, with its open gateways, belongs to the fleeting realm of air and smoke, the **mouth** is the powerful tool for the solid matter of the earth. The word derives from the Old High German *munt* and the Proto-Germanic *munþaz* (English *mouth*), ultimately tracing back to the Indo-European root *menth₂-*, which originally meant **"to turn, churn, chew, grind"** (in Latin, this became *mentum*, the chin moving during chewing) and is also related to *men-* ("to protrude," as in Latin *mons* for mountain). The mouth was thus defined by its alert, active work: the powerful breaking down and grinding of earthly food. *(A curious misconception in the history of language is the existence of words like "guardian" or "the early bird catches the worm"—they don't actually refer to our eating organ, but rather to the extinct Old High German word "die Munt" for "protective hand," related to the Latin *manus*.)

Inside this grinding mechanism rests the **tongue** (Old High German *zunga*, Proto-Germanic *tungōn*), which traces back to the Indo-European *dṇg̑hū-*. The Latin equivalent shows how closely language and physical action are intertwined: The tongue was originally called *dingua* in Old Latin. However, in everyday speech, the Romans unconsciously transformed this into *lingua*—because they phonetically adapted the organ to the verb *lingere* ("to lick"). The biological function shaped the name.

The journey of the word **taste** is even more adventurous. It originates from the Old High German *smacken* (11th century) and the Indo-European root *smeg-* (“to taste”). But in the Germanic language area, the word took a fascinating detour: it migrated from the mouth up to the nose and acquired the meaning “to smell, to have a fragrance.” Anyone listening in southern Germany or Switzerland today can still hear this ancient echo when they hear: “It tastes like smoke” or “The flower tastes good.” Only in Late Middle High German did the word return to its original place on the tongue in High German—linguistic proof that our ancestors intuitively knew how inextricably the openings of the nose and mouth work together in the pharynx.

### The Skin and Feeling: The Protective Hut of Being

Our largest sensory organ, the **skin**, reveals at its core a deep need for security. Through the Old High German *hūt* and the Proto-Germanic *hūdi-*, it traces back to the Indo-European root *(s)keu-*, which means **"to envelop, cover, conceal."** Words that we hardly associate with our skin today grew from precisely this same root: the **hat** (the covering of the head), the **hut** (the protective shelter), and the caring verb **to guard**. The English *hide* (both "animal hide" and "to conceal"), the Latin *cutis*, and the Greek *kytos* ("cavity, cell") also belong to this family. From a linguistic-historical perspective, our skin is thus our most personal shelter—the first sanctuary that protects our innermost being.

To make contact with the world from within this protective shelter, we use two verbs: **to feel** and **to touch**. While the Old High German word *fuolen* (to feel) derives from the root *pāl-* (“to touch, gently strike, stir,” related to the Latin *palpare* = to caress), the Old French word *taster* (to taste) is a later addition, originating from Old French (*taster*), which comes from the Vulgar Latin *taxitāre* (“to touch intensely, to examine”). Remarkably, this very *taster* also migrated to England, where the “examining touch” became a “testing taste with the mouth”—the modern English *to taste*. Once again, the senses merge at their linguistic roots.

The final word is the **ear** (Old High German *ōra*, Proto-Germanic *\*ōraz-*), which is related to the Latin *auris*, the Greek *ous*, and the English *ear* via the Indo-European root *\*ōus-*. Linguists even suspect that the German word *Auge* (eye) only acquired its distinctive "Au-" at the beginning through the influence of the *Ohr* (Proto-Germanic *\*auzō-*), because for centuries both organs were referred to in unison as a twin pair – "eyes and ears."

Here, the deepest connection between the eye and the ear is revealed: If we look even further back into the Proto-Indo-European language, we encounter the common root ***\*(s)keuH-***. Through a so-called *s-mobile* (a movable *s* at the beginning of a word), this primal root split into two branches: Without the *s*, our **hören** (to hear, to perceive attentively, to listen, to hear acoustically) developed; with the *s*, **schauen** (to look, to take in the world visually) arose.

In the earliest days of language, there was therefore no separation between seeing and hearing. Both verbs originated from the same word for **pure mindfulness** – for the undivided, alert pause in the here and now, when a branch bends or a sound resounds in the stillness of the forest.


When we reflect today on the roots of our senses, we remember that our body is not an isolated vessel, but a living resonating chamber – built to follow the world, to guard its fire in sleep, to grind its fruits, to dwell in its hut, and to listen to it with an alert mind.
