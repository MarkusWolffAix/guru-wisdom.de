# The body's memory
## How the etymology of our five senses reveals a forgotten worldview

Language is far more than a mere tool for transmitting information. It is a living archive, a profound memory of humankind, in which the experiences, worldviews, and moments of wonder of our most distant ancestors are preserved. When we speak today of our five senses—sight, smell, taste, touch, and hearing—we use words that have been polished over millennia like pebbles in a riverbed. Yet beneath their smooth, everyday surface lies an archaic power.

In the modern world, we often understand perception as a passive process: stimuli stream in on us, and our bodies register them. However, a look at etymology, the origin of our words, paints a completely different picture. For the people of early Indo-European and Germanic times, perception was not a silent receiving, but an active movement out into the world. Even the overarching word **sense** (Old High German *sin* ) goes back to the Indo-European root **sent-* , which originally meant "to take a direction, to follow a trail, to travel." To perceive with the senses, therefore, meant in the truest sense of the word, to set out on a journey and trace the paths of life.

### The eye and seeing: The art of tracking

Our most important gateway to the world is the **eye** . It originates from the Old High German * *ouga** (from the 8th century) and the Germanic **augan-** , which in turn go back to the ancient Indo-European root **okʷ-* * or **okū-** ("to see"). This root is so fundamental that it runs like a common thread through the European language family – from the Latin * *oculus* * through the Gothic * *augō* * to the English * *eye** .

Things get interesting when we look at the corresponding verb: **sehen (to see** ). The trail leads us through the Old High German *sehan* and the Proto-Germanic **sehwan-* to the Indo-European root **sekʷ-* . Its original meaning was "to notice, to follow." It is closely related to the Latin *sequi* ("to follow"). For our ancestors, seeing was not a static fixation, but a dynamic accompaniment: The eye attaches itself to the trail of a migrating animal, the flight of a bird, or the course of the celestial bodies and *follows* them through space.

### The nose and the sense of smell: The gateway to the breath of life and the night watchman at the fire

Hardly any word in the Indo-European language family has survived the millennia as unscathed as the **nose** . From the Indo-European **nās-* through the Old Indian *nā́sā* and the Latin *nasus* to the Old High German *nasa* and the English *nose* , the sound remained almost unchanged – like a rock in the surf of language change (the word even lives on in geographical names for promontories like *Blankenese* or *Loch Ness* ).

But even more fascinating than the age of the word is its original grammar: In the original language *, *nās-* was a dual form, representing two people. Our ancestors didn't name the nose after the single visible cartilaginous protrusion on the face, but after the invisible cavity: **"the two nostrils."** For ancient cultures, these two openings were the sacred gateways of the breath of life—whether as *prana* in Indian Sanskrit, as the divine breath of life in the creation story, or as *pneuma* and *spiritus* in antiquity, where breath, mind, and soul were still one and the same word. The nose is the only threshold of our body that is never closed.

It is precisely at this ever-open threshold that the deep secret of the verb **"riechen** " (to smell) is revealed. It originates from the Old High German *"riohhan* " (8th century) and the Proto-Germanic **reuk-a-* . Its original meaning was simply **"to smoke, to vaporize, to give off smoke"** – it is the direct linguistic sibling of our noun " **Rauch"** (smoke) (preserved in English as *"to reek* ").

But why did the word for fire smoke merge with our nasal perception? The answer lies in the dark stillness of the night. When early humans slept in their caves or huts, all the senses withdrew: eyelids were closed, mouths sealed, and the waking mind extinguished. Only one sense kept tireless watch at the two open gateways of breath: the nose, connected by a direct nerve pathway to the oldest survival centers of our brain. When the vital hut fire threatened to go out or got out of control, it sent its invisible messenger through the darkness—smoke. Because the nose is the organ that remains active even in the unconsciousness of sleep and hears the fire's warning call, the word for "smoking" became, over the course of the Middle Ages, the word for our deepest, most unconscious sensory perception: "smelling."

### Mouth, tongue, and taste: The earthly mill

While the nose, with its open openings, belongs to the fleeting realm of air and smoke, the **mouth** is the powerful tool for the solid matter of the earth. The word derives from the Old High German * *munt** and the Proto-Germanic **munþaz** (English *mouth* ), ultimately tracing back to the Indo-European root **menth₂-* , which originally meant **"to turn, churn, chew, grind"** (in Latin this became **mentum* *, the chin moving during chewing) and is also related to **men-** ("to protrude," as in Latin **mons** for mountain). Thus, the mouth was defined by its alert, active work: the powerful breaking down and grinding of earthly nourishment. *(Incidentally, a curious error in the history of language are words like "guardian" or "the early bird catches the worm" – they do not contain our eating organ, but the extinct Old High German word "die Munt" for "protective hand", related to Latin manus).*

Inside this grinding mechanism rests the **tongue** (Old High German *zunga* , Proto-Germanic **tungōn* ), which derives from the Indo-European **dṇg̑hū-* . The close connection between language and physical action is illustrated by its Latin counterpart: Originally, the tongue was called **dingua* in Old Latin. However, in everyday speech, the Romans unconsciously transformed this *into *lingua* – because they phonetically adapted the organ to the verb **lingere** ("to lick"). The biological function shaped the name.

Even more adventurous is the journey of the word " **geschmacken** " (to taste). It originates from the Old High German " *smacken* " (11th century) and the Indo-European root **smeg-* ("to taste"). But in the Germanic language area, the word undertook a fascinating detour: it migrated from the mouth up to the nose and took on the meaning "to smell, to have a fragrance." Anyone listening in southern Germany or Switzerland today can still hear this ancient echo when they hear: "Es schmeckt nach Rauch" (It tastes like smoke) or "Die Blume schmeckt gut" (The flower tastes good). Only in Late Middle High German did the word return to its original place on the tongue in High German – linguistic proof that our ancestors intuitively knew how inextricably the openings of the nose and mouth work together in the pharynx.

### The skin and the sense of touch: The protective shelter of being

Our largest sensory organ, the **skin** , reveals at its very root a deep need for security. Through the Old High German * *hūt** and the Proto-Germanic **hūdi-*,* it traces back to the Indo-European root *** (s)keu- **, meaning **"to envelop, cover, conceal."** From precisely this same root grew words that we today hardly associate with our body's surface: the **hat** (the covering of the head), the **hut** (the protective shelter), and the caring verb * **behüten** * (to guard/protect). The English *hide** (both "animal hide" and "to conceal"), the Latin * *cutis** , and the Greek * *kytos** ("hollow, cell") also belong to this family. From a linguistic-historical perspective, our skin is thus our most personal shelter—the first sanctuary that protects our innermost being.

To make contact with the world from within the protective confines of our hut, we use two verbs: **to feel** and **to touch** . While *"feel"* (Old High German * *fuolen* *) derives from the root **pāl-** ("to touch, gently strike, stir," related to Latin * *palpare* * = to caress), *"touch"* is a later addition from Old French (* *taster* *), which comes from Vulgar Latin **taxitāre** ("to touch intensely, to examine"). Remarkably, this very **taster** also migrated to England, where the "examining touch" became a "testing taste with the mouth"—the modern English * *to* taste*. Once again, the senses merge at their linguistic roots.

### The ear and hearing: The common root of mindfulness

The final word is the **ear** (Old High German *ōra* , Proto-Germanic **ōraz-* ), which is related via the Indo-European root **ōus-* to the Latin *auris* , the Greek *ous* , and the English *ear* . Linguists even suspect that the German word *Auge (eye* ) only acquired its distinctive "Au-" at the beginning through the influence of the *ear* (Proto-Germanic **auzō-* ), because for centuries both organs were referred to in unison as a twin pair – "eyes and ears".

Here, the deepest connection between the eye and the ear is also revealed: If we look even further back into the Proto-Indo-European language, we encounter the common root ***\* (s)keuH-** *. Through a so-called *s-mobile* (a movable *s* at the beginning of a word), this original root split into two branches: Without the *s* , our **word for hearing** developed ( *\*kous-* , to perceive attentively, to listen, to eavesdrop acoustically); with the *s,* it gave rise to the word for **looking** ( *\*skeuH-* , to take in the world visually). 

In the earliest days of language, there was therefore no separation between seeing and hearing. Both verbs originated from the same word for **pure mindfulness** – for the undivided, alert pause in the here and now, when a branch bends or a sound resounds in the silence of the forest. 

When we reflect today on the roots of our senses, we remember that our body is not an isolated vessel, but a living resonating chamber – built to follow the world, to guard its fire in sleep, to grind its fruits, to dwell in its hut, and to listen to it with an alert mind.
