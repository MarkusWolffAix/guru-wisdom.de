# Aachen thermal springs
## The hot legacy of Earth's history

This summary documents the origin, the physical laws and the historical significance of the hottest springs in Central Europe.

### 1. The origin and locations
The Aachen thermal springs are not formed by direct volcanism, but by a fascinating geological cycle. Rainwater seeps into the ground in the Eifel region, sinks to depths of up to 4,000 meters, and is heated there by natural geothermal energy (geothermal gradient) to well over 100 °C. Enormous pressure then rapidly forces it back to the surface.

* **Aachen Springs (city center):** Here you will find springs such as the Elisenbrunnen and the Kaiserquelle. The water emerges at approximately 52 °C and has a high sulfur content.
* **Burtscheider Quellenzug:** Here lie the hottest springs, such as the Rosenquelle, with temperatures of up to 74 °C.

### 2. The geological necessity: The Earth's fissures
Without the geological fault zones (the Aachen and Burtscheid thrust faults), there would be no thermal water in Aachen. These faults act like a "speed elevator" to the surface. Without them, the water would remain trapped deep underground, flowing horizontally, cooling down in the process, and emerging elsewhere merely as an ordinary cold-water spring.

### 3. The riddle of 100 degrees: pressure and boiling point
At a depth of 4,000 meters, there is a hydrostatic pressure of approximately 300 to 400 bar (caused by the kilometer-high column of water and the weight of the rock). Following the **principle of a pressure cooker,** this pressure raises the boiling point of water. As a result, the water remains liquid despite temperatures exceeding 100 °C and does not turn to steam.

### 4. The Engine: The Eifel region and the principle of communicating vessels
The system is driven by the difference in altitude. The Eifel region (forming area) lies at approximately 500–700 m above sea level, while the Aachen basin lies at only about 150 m above sea level. 

* **Thought experiment 1: What if the Eifel region were flat?**
  If the Eifel region were at sea level (like Aachen), there would be no pressure difference. The water would not rise. The phenomenon would cease completely.

* **Thought experiment 2: What if the Eifel region were only half as high (approx. 350 m)?**
  If the Eifel region were only 350 meters high, the remaining pressure head would be approximately 200 meters (350 m minus 150 m in Aachen). This would have three concrete consequences:
  1. **The water continues to bubble (but with less force):** A gradient of 200 meters is still physically sufficient to push the water upwards according to the principle of communicating vessels, however the artesian pressure would be significantly lower.
  2. **From express elevator to slow train:** Due to the lower pressure from above, the flow rate decreases drastically. The water takes significantly longer to travel from a depth of 4,000 meters.
  3. **The loss of heat:** Because the water rises much more slowly, it spends significantly more time in the cooler, upper rock layers. It releases its heat to the rock. 
  **Conclusion:** Instead of gushing forth from the ground at steaming temperatures of 52°C to 74°C, Aachen would only have **lukewarm springs (approx. 25°C to 35°C)** . The spectacular steaming in winter and the title of "Central Europe's hottest springs" would be lost to the city.

### 5. The formation of the fissures (Variscan orogeny)
Around 300 to 320 million years ago, continental plates collided to form the supercontinent Pangaea. During this time (the Carboniferous period), the rock in what is now the Aachen region was folded and eventually fractured. Older rock layers were thrust over younger ones. These ancient "scars" in the Earth's crust now form the pathways for water to rise.

### 6. The 5 elements of the Aachen springs
* **Earth:** The folded rock and the necessary pressure height of the Eifel region.
* **Water:** The supple traveler that takes the path of least resistance.
* **Fire:** The hidden geothermal heat in the Earth's interior.
* **Air:** The released water vapor and sulfur gas at the surface.
* **Ether:** The invisible pressure and the natural laws of physics.

### 7. History and naming: Aquae Granni
Even the Celts and Romans revered the springs. The Romans dedicated the place to the healing god Apollo Grannus and called the settlement **"Aquae Granni"** (the waters of Grannus). Over the centuries, this Latin name evolved into the modern name **Aachen** . The characteristic sulfurous smell ("rotten eggs") is caused by hydrogen sulfide, which is dissolved from minerals such as pyrite (fool's gold) in the ancient rock.
