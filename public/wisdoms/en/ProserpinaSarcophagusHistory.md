# Proserpina sarcophagus
## Myth, Christian reinterpretation, and Charlemagne

### Unabridged summary: The Proserpina sarcophagus

This text examines the Roman Proserpina sarcophagus (3rd century AD) located in the Aachen Cathedral treasury from three main perspectives: the ancient origin myth, its Christian-biblical reinterpretation, and its historical reuse as a tomb for Charlemagne.

#### 1. The ancient myth: The Rape of Proserpina
The central motif of the relief is the Roman explanatory myth for the seasons and the cycle of life:
* **The robbery:** Pluto, the ruler of the underworld, abducts the unsuspecting Proserpina on his chariot to his dark realm.
* **The mother's despair:** Ceres, the goddess of agriculture, searches so desperately for her daughter that she neglects her duties and the harvests on earth wither.
* **The compromise:** Because Proserpina ate pomegranate seeds (the food of the dead) in the underworld, she is bound to the realm of the dead. Jupiter brokers a compromise: Proserpina spends spring and summer on Earth (nature flourishes) and autumn and winter in the underworld (nature dies).
* **Meaning:** On ancient graves, the myth offered comfort, as it symbolically represented the hope for life after death and constant rebirth.

#### 2. Biblical Analogies and Christian Reinterpretation
In the Middle Ages, the *practice of interpretatio Christiana* was applied: pagan cultural heritage was reinterpreted within the context of the Christian faith, as it was seen as containing premonitions of divine truth. The analogies are strong:
* **Resurrection:** Proserpina's return from the underworld was understood as a parallel to Jesus Christ's descent into the realm of the dead and his subsequent resurrection.
* **The grain of wheat:** The winter sojourn of the harvest goddess's daughter underground is similar to Jesus' biblical metaphor (John 12:24): Only when the grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies can it bring forth new life.
* **Mater Dolorosa:** Ceres' profound grief for her child is strongly reminiscent of the Christian iconography of the grieving Mary (Mother of Sorrows) beneath the cross.
* **The pomegranate:** This fruit has been completely reinterpreted in Christian terms – as a symbol for the Church (many seeds united in one bowl), eternal life and the cleansing blood of Christ.

#### 3. Historical reuse: The tomb of Charlemagne
The use of the pagan sarcophagus in 814 for the Christian emperor Charlemagne is a prime example of *spolia reuse* (reuse of ancient plunder or spoils):
* **Logistics:** The transport of the fine Carrara marble, weighing many tons, from Rome to Aachen across the Alps and rivers was extremely complex and a huge status symbol.
* **Ideology:** Charlemagne pursued the *Renovatio imperii Romanorum* (renewal of the Roman Empire). His burial in a genuine Roman sarcophagus reinforced his political claim as the direct successor of the ancient Caesars.
* **Practical implementation:** The sarcophagus was cleaned and consecrated before use to rid it of "pagan filth." The original lid, which is now missing, likely fell victim to this process, as it often bore pagan inscriptions.
* **History of the burial site:** Charlemagne's sarcophagus did not serve him for eternity. The tomb was opened by later emperors (including Otto III and Frederick Barbarossa). In the 12th and 13th centuries, Charlemagne's remains were transferred to the golden Shrine of Charlemagne, and the empty sarcophagus later became part of the cathedral treasury.
