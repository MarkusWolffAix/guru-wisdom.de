# The unity of opposites
## The Eagle, the Serpent and the Ring of Eternity

Since the dawn of humankind, myths, traditions, and philosophical writings have testified to a universal image that resides within us all like a deep cellular memory: the golden eagle and the serpent. This pair of opposites embodies the most elemental forces of existence. The eagle represents the vast sky, the ascending spirit, light, and visionary transcendence. The serpent, on the other hand, roots us in the earth; it is matter, transformation, the unconscious, and deep, instinctive wisdom. When these two primal forces meet, the absolute cosmic order is revealed—be it in the bitter struggle of duality or in the perfect peace of their union.

The way in which these energies connect can be viewed at three central points of contact, which, depending on the wisdom tradition, illuminate different facets of being.

In Friedrich Nietzsche's work *Thus Spoke Zarathustra,* we encounter the philosophical level. Here, the eagle, as the proudest of animals, and the serpent, as the wisest, are not enemies, but allies of the wise hermit Zarathustra. They unite at the eagle's neck, where the serpent closes its ring around him in deep friendship. This ring symbolizes the "eternal recurrence of the same"—the absolute affirmation of life in all its highs and lows, far removed from linear time.

The image of the ring, in particular, elevates this connection beyond the profane struggle. Whether as *the ouroboros* of antiquity—the snake biting its own tail, symbolizing infinity and constant renewal—or as the eternal cycle of breath on the yoga mat, the ring dissolves the limitations of the mind. It evokes the vibration of Ohm, the universal sound that knows no beginning and no end. When the eagle wears this ring, it teaches us that the highest spiritual realization lies not in escaping the world, but in unconditional surrender to its cyclical rhythm.

Let's shine a light on this ring from three different dimensions:

**1. The philosophical dimension: Eternal recurrence**
For Nietzsche, this ring is the absolute center of his philosophy. He calls it the "eternal recurrence of the same." The ring whispers to us: Time is not a straight line running from A to B, but an eternal cycle. The snake, which closes to form the ring, affirms life in all its highs (eagle) and lows (serpent) so unconditionally that it would want to repeat every single moment exactly as it is, for all eternity. It is the highest expression of love for life—an unconditional "yes" to existence.

**2. The mythological dimension: The Ouroboros**
In comparative mythology, whether in the ancient Greek mysteries or the early Sumerian writings, we know this symbol as the ouroboros – the snake biting its own tail. It symbolizes infinity, creation from nothing, and the fact that the end is always also a new beginning. When the eagle (the celestial spirit) carries this ouroboros, it means that the highest spiritual knowledge lies not in escaping the world, but in recognizing its perfect, cyclical nature.

**3. The spiritual dimension: The eternal sound**
When we bring this ring into our own practice, for example onto the yoga mat, it represents the cycle of breath. It is the moment when inhalation becomes exhalation and vice versa – the absolute stillness in the gap between. It is comparable to the vibration of Ohm: a sound that has no true beginning and no end, but rather, as an eternal, vibrating wave, permeates and holds together the entire universe. The snake as a ring around the eagle's neck is the connection between deepest grounding and highest consciousness in an infinite flow.

Zarathustra, standing as a solitary seeker on the mountaintops, embodies all these insights. He has left behind old dogmas to find perfect balance: deeply rooted in the harsh, earthly realm, yet completely free in spirit. The eagle and the serpent ultimately teach us that the struggle within us only ends when we cease to pit heaven against earth. Whoever recognizes these two forces as allies finds creative power within themselves—and becomes a true mother to this world, transcending duality in the ring of eternity.

**The Aztec Vision (The Mexican Flag)**
[image:EagleAndSerpentRing2]
The image on the Mexican flag is based on the founding myth of the capital city Tenochtitlán (present-day Mexico City). The sun god Huitzilopochtli prophesied to the Aztecs that they would find their eternal homeland where an eagle sits on a prickly pear cactus devouring a snake.
The deeper meaning: For the indigenous peoples of Mesoamerica, this was not the victory of "good" (eagle) over "evil" (snake). The eagle represented solar power, the sky, and light. The snake (coatl) stood for earth, water, and fertility. From the eagle's beak often emerged the symbol atl-tlachinolli (burning water), which the Spanish conquerors later often misinterpreted simply as a snake. It was the symbol of the ultimate union of opposites—fire and water, sky and earth—from which a high culture could even arise.

**Mesopotamia: The Tree of Life (Etana Myth)**
[image:EagleAndSerpentRing3]
The myth of Etana is found on ancient cylinder seals from the Sumerian and Akkadian periods. Here, an eagle and a snake live together in a large tree (often interpreted as a Huluppu tree). The eagle nests in the crown, the snake in the roots.
The deeper meaning: They swear an oath of friendship before the sun god Shamash. But the eagle breaks the oath and devours the snake's children, whereupon a bitter battle ensues. This represents a phase in which the human spirit (the soaring eagle) loses its connection to its earthly roots (the snake) and becomes arrogant. It is a reminder that intellect cannot survive without the instincts of the earth.

**Garuda and the Nagas**
[image:EagleAndSerpentRing4]
In Hindu mythology and the deep roots of yoga, we encounter Garuda, the giant celestial eagle (Vishnu's mount), and the Nagas, the wise serpent beings of the underworld. They are considered eternal enemies.
The deeper meaning: In yogic alchemy, they represent the energies within our body. The eagle is prana (the upward-flowing life energy, the light, the spirit), the serpent is apana (the downward-flowing energy, the grounding force), or also kundalini. The serpent, as kundalini energy, rests at the base of our spine (Muladhara Chakra), while the eagle represents the far-sightedness of our mind (Ajna Chakra). The third point of connection, the actual alchemical furnace, is the heart (Anahata Chakra). Only here, in the center of our chest, do the ascending (prana) and descending (apana) energies unite. The "struggle" between the two is actually the process of inner purification. Only when these two energies cease to fight and unite in the heart center does true spiritual awakening occur.

**Ancient Greece: The Omen in the Iliad**
[image:EagleAndSerpentRing5]
The image appears in Homer's Iliad when an eagle flies over the battlefield with a bleeding, still-living snake in its talons. The snake fights back, biting the eagle in the chest, and the eagle drops it.
The deeper meaning: Here, the eagle represents the will of Zeus (the celestial order), while the serpent represents the chthonic (earth-bound) primal forces. The serpent's descent shows that the earth cannot be completely dominated by the heavens.

**Eagle and snake at Göbekli Tepe**
[image:EagleAndSerpentRing6]
At the Neolithic site of Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey, a window opens to the soul of early humanity. When we gaze upon the massive, T-shaped pillars of this over twelve-thousand-year-old site, we are beholding nothing less than the first dreams, spiritual ideas, and fears of our ancestors carved in stone. It is the very beginning of our collective memory. 

It is no coincidence that the eagle – or rather the vulture – and the snake appear there as central and most frequently depicted animal motifs. In these two animals, we encounter one of the oldest and deepest dualisms in human history. It is a fascinating symbolism that would resurface millennia later in countless cultures around the globe to describe the eternal polarity of our existence.

In the minds of many early cultures, large birds of prey, such as eagles and vultures, were far more than mere animals; they were companions of the dead. In ritual burial practices, like sky burials, these birds carried the remains of the deceased into the air. The vulture did not act as a simple scavenger, but as a carrier and transformer of souls. It took in the earthly, the transient, and raised it to the heavens. Thus, the bird represents the upper world, the spirit, the boundless expanse of the sky, and the final letting go of the material body.

The snake, the guardian, forms the perfect archaic counterpoint. It is deeply rooted in the earth and the underworld. It crawls on its belly, disappears into dark, cool crevices in the earth, and withdraws from the light. At the same time, through the act of shedding its skin, it is a universal and powerful symbol of rebirth, healing, and an indestructible life force. On the T-shaped pillars of Göbekli Tepe, snakes are often depicted in flowing, downward movements. They represent the earthbound, the profound mystery, fertility, and the raw, untamed power of matter.

When we now consider both animals united on the same stone pillars, we witness perhaps the earliest expression of a cosmic balance, the comic dance: heaven and earth, spirit and matter, death and rebirth. It is the eternal dance of polarities, captured in the hard stone of the Anatolian landscape. Stone Age man likely positioned himself precisely between these two immense forces—firmly rooted to the earth like the serpent, but blessed with a consciousness that could soar freely to the heavens like the eagle.

This symbolism still touches something very deep within us today. It reminds us that human life has always been a balancing act between material reality and spiritual longing. Göbekli Tepe is therefore not just a monument of the past, but an eternal mirror of human nature, teaching us to understand the polarities of life not as opposites, but as inseparable parts of a great, cosmic whole.
